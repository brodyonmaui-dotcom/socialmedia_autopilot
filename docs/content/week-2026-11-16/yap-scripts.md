- **Alt hook:** The most expensive emotion in trading isn't greed.
- **Anchor:** Loss aversion: losses loom larger than gains, roughly twice as strongly (Kahneman & Tversky, Prospect Theory 1979; Tversky & Kahneman 1992). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_aversion · **verified** (say "roughly")
- **Close:** You can't stop feeling the loss. You can stop letting it vote.

**Script**
<!-- script -->
Your brain charges you double for every loss, and that's the whole reason you can't follow your own rules.

There's a thing in psychology called loss aversion. Kahneman and Tversky found that losses hit roughly twice as hard as gains of the same size.

So when a trade goes against me, I'm not feeling a small red number. I'm feeling twice that. And twice that is enough to make me move a stop, or hold something I already decided to close.

It's like a scale that's been rigged before you ever step on it. You can argue with the reading all day. It's still rigged.

The fix isn't being a braver person. It's not more confidence, and it's not some mindset quote taped to the monitor. It's deciding the exit before the trade, when nothing is on the line yet, and then treating the version of me in the trade like he's not allowed to change the plan.

You can't stop feeling the loss. You can stop letting it vote.

- **Alt hook:** Judging your decisions by the result is how you train yourself to gamble.
- **Anchor:** "Resulting": judging the quality of a decision by the quality of its outcome (Annie Duke, *Thinking in Bets*, 2018). https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Annie_Duke · **verified**
- **Close:** Did you make a good decision, or did you just get paid for a bad one?

**Script**
<!-- script -->
A green day can be the worst trade you made all week.

Annie Duke, the poker player, has a name for the mistake. She calls it resulting. It's judging how good a decision was by how it turned out.

And trading is built to make you do it. You break your rules, the market bails you out, and now your brain files that as a good decision. So you do it again.

It's like running a red light and getting home fine. Getting home fine doesn't make it a good idea. It just means it didn't cost you yet.

So I don't grade my days by P and L anymore. Not on the good days, and not on the bad ones either. A red day where I followed every rule is a better day than a green one where I didn't. That still feels wrong to write down. It's still true.

Did you make a good decision, or did you just get paid for a bad one?

## #5 · Process > results
- **Hook:** The moment I made a P&L goal, I started becoming a worse trader.
- **Alt hook:** Any number you chase long enough stops measuring what you wanted it to.
- **Anchor:** Goodhart's Law, as Marilyn Strathern put it (1997): "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." https://www.gwern.net/doc/statistics/decision/1997-strathern.pdf · **verified with correction** (credit Strathern's wording, Goodhart's idea)
- **Close:** Chase the number and you'll get the number. You might not get better.

**Script**
<!-- script -->
The moment I made a P&L goal, I started becoming a worse trader.

There's an idea called Goodhart's Law. The way the anthropologist Marilyn Strathern phrased it is: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.

P and L was supposed to tell me if my process was working. Then I set a weekly number, and it stopped being a measurement. It became a thing to hit. So on a Thursday when I was short of it, I'd take trades I would never take on a Monday.

It's like a student who studies for the test instead of the subject. The grade goes up. The understanding doesn't.

So now the targets are things I control. Did I wait for my setup. Did I size it right. Did I stop when I said I'd stop. Not the money. The money is just the receipt.

Chase the number and you'll get the number. You might not get better.

## #6 · Ego and self-criticism
- **Hook:** Being hard on yourself after a bad day is not discipline, it's just a slower way to quit.
- **Alt hook:** The harshest voice in your head isn't the one making you better.
- **Anchor:** Breines & Chen (2012, *PSPB*): after a failed test, people prompted toward self-compassion spent more time studying for the retake than those prompted toward self-esteem or nothing. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22645164/ · **verified** (lab study; say "one study")
- **Close:** You can hold yourself to a standard without holding yourself in contempt.

**Script**
<!-- script -->
Being hard on yourself after a bad day is not discipline, it's just a slower way to quit.

I used to think the self-criticism was the engine. Like if I stopped tearing myself apart after a loss, I'd get soft.

Then I found this study by Breines and Chen. People failed a test, and the ones who were nudged to be kind to themselves about it studied longer for the next one. Not less. Longer.

Which makes sense when you think about it. If every mistake comes with a beating, you stop looking at your mistakes. You don't journal the bad trade, you don't rewatch the bad set, you just want it over.

It's the difference between a coach and a critic. Both see the same mistake. Only one of them makes you want to come back tomorrow.

Self-compassion isn't letting yourself off the hook. It's not pretending the day was fine. It's making the mistake safe enough to look at.

You can hold yourself to a standard without holding yourself in contempt.

**Script**
<!-- script -->
Most people don't lose money because they pick bad trades, they lose it because they can't stand being wrong.

There's a well-documented pattern in finance called the disposition effect. People tend to sell their winners too early and hold their losers too long.

And it's not a math problem. It's an ego problem. Closing a winner feels like proof I was right, so I grab it. Closing a loser means admitting I was wrong, so I wait. And wait. Hoping it comes back so I don't have to admit anything.

It's like staying in an argument you already know you lost, just because leaving would mean saying it out loud.

I'm not immune to this. It's most of what my bad days have in common. Not bad analysis. Not bad luck. Just me protecting my ego with real money.

The stop isn't there to protect my account. It's there to protect me from me.

Being wrong costs a little. Refusing to be wrong costs everything.
